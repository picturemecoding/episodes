### Plot Synopsis (Books 1, 2, and the novel *Network Effect*)

*All Systems Red* (2017):

Our narrator and main character is a Security Unit (SecUnit) rented from a company in a hypercapitalist galaxy called “Corporation Rim” where these scientific visitors to planets have to take on insurance contracts where they’re mandated to have security units. SecUnit is a robot and we meet after he has hacked his governor module which would otherwise fry his brain if he does not obey the humans he reports to. SecUnits are slave labor but Murderbot has free will in other words. Even so, the only activity he *wants* to do is watch his favorite soap operas including his favorite show: *the Rise and Fall of Sanctuary Moon*.

The people he is working for are from Preservation (“Pres Aux”) and they believe in autonomy and free expressions of individuality and other woke shit. They live outside of the hypercapitalist Corporation Rim and the practices and procedures of the corporation rim (such as indentured servitude) are foreign and abhorrent to them. (Alexander Skarsgard who plays Murderbot in the Apple TV show based on the book described the PresAux team in an interview I saw as “basically a bunch of space hippies”.) The leader of this group is Dr Mensah and Murderbot develops a special bond with her that evolves over the next four books.

Eventually there are some battles and some action and the PresAux team discovers that SecUnit is rogue with a hacked governor module but they respond to him as something like an independent, autonomous being which seems to unsettle it? They also discover that he calls himself Murderbot (why?) and that he had previously murdered 57 people on another contract.

*Artificial Condition* (2018):

Murderbot discretely takes off from the PresAux team and goes on a journey to find out more about his murdering of humans. In order to travel, he makes these deals using bot communication channels with autonomous bot pilots, which he treats as kind of like dumb programs like how you would treat an ATM or something if it chirped at you. He usually hacks them or offers to share all of his downloaded media (soap operas) with them. When he’s close to his destination he boards one particular ship, which turns out to be an extremely powerful unmanned research ship that turns out to have this massively powerful artificial intelligence on it and it’s extremely nosy not to mention vaguely threatening). He calls it ART for “asshole research transport.” But they become friends! ART helps him modify his appearance and mannerisms to pass as a human.

*Network Effect* (2020):

In the beginning of this novel, Murderbot is returning from a scientific mission with Dr Mensah’s daughter Amena and other PresAux members when their ship is attacked and Murderbot and Amena are dragged aboard the attacking ship which is populated by these gray humanoids with what’s described as a bizarre language and bad manners. They turn out to have been (at one point) humans exposed to alien remnants, but then the ship gets sucked into a wormhole.

While wandering through the ship killing hostiles Murderbot realizes that the ship is ART and he’s been erased, which is a huge emotional blow for him.

He ends up restoring ART and they argue a bunch and ART wants Murderbot to go rescue his crew on a hostile planet with alien remnants and lots of bad guys.

Along the way he and ART replicate Murderbot and the narration splits between main Murderbot and replicated Murderbot. Amena says “you and ART are making a baby!” and Murderbot is horrified by this.

## Some Themes

### Why is the Murderbot Character Compelling

[Mike] My takes. First is that Murderbot is effectively enslaved and then frees itself (hacks its governor module). Everyone wants to hack their governor module (yes, i understand this sounds vaguely like a euphemism…), by which i mean that sentient beings want to exercise their free will, but often don’t because of constraints like money, social mores, family obligations, etc. Second, the sort of people who like these books are probably the sort of people who like Murderbot’s sarcasm and anti-social behavior. In interviews i’ve read that readers interpret its behavior to be non-neurotypical. I didn’t really get that, but i do get the “please leave me alone so i can watch my shows” vibe. Finally, it’s compelling that, while Murderbot is not enthusiastic about being violent, it *is* competent in its security role (even if it often claims not to be). Put another way, it is interesting as both a conscious being and also a dangerous weapon.

[Amy] Re: Murderbot being neurodivergent, I think support for that reading is in things like: (1) it not being able to make eye contact and not wanting to be touched, (2) having emotions that it can’t identify or articulate (it has “an emotion” or things happen in its organic parts), (3) extreme competency in one specific area and zero competency in others, (4) inability to decipher what human facial expressions are meant to convey. Etc. Re: governor modules, things get pretty interesting in Network Effect and System Collapse when MB starts “freeing” other SecUnits. MB has a lot of internalized fear and loathing of SecUnits and it’s interesting to watch that play out. Dovetails with how much it doesn’t want to be perceived by humans because it hates itself so much.

Martha Wells was asked if she was specifically thinking about neurodivergence in the *Scientific American* interview :

I knew I had problems with anxiety and things like that, but I didn’t know I probably had autism. I didn’t know a lot of other things until writing this particular story and then having people talk to me about it. They’re like, “How did you manage to portray neurodiversity like this?” And I’m thinking, “That’s just how my brain works. This is the way I think people think.”

[erik] I like how conflicted Murderbot is in trying to understand its own reactions to its relationships. It has genuine, deep emotions as well that I find really compelling. There’s a moment in Network Effect where it realizes that a SecUnit on a space port was intentionally left behind by some hostiles and once its leader gets far enough away its brain gets fried and melts out of its face or something and this is profoundly disturbing for Murderbot to see. That reflection on self and sadness over something tragic happening to someone you’ve never met but can relate to: I find those kinds of responses totally compelling in this character.

Plus, he’s so damn funny. His disgust over human behaviors such as sex and using the bathroom, the way he makes typical human things seem bizarre and disgusting is funny.

His savviness about the super cynical world of the Corporation Rim and they way he sardonically characterizes the PresAux team’s naivete about the “real world” around them is so funny.

Also, he’s discovering the self, becoming self-aware. BUT he doesn’t know what he wants to do with himself so he just watches soap operas: is that relatable or what?

### What the Murderbot Intelligence is Like

[Mike] Wells does a good job of combining organic intelligence and machine intelligence. Murderbot expresses frustration, disdain, admiration, insecurity; but also computational ability that exceeds human capability. For example, it deploys its drones to give it more views of a situation and Wells points out that a human could not simultaneously monitor all of these views (eg, imagine VR goggles or smart glasses?). It has the ability to run background tasks or suspend computations (Wells uses the term “backburner”, i think?). I also like the way that she makes clear the difference between Murderbot and ART.

There are some aspects of MB’s operation that i really like and some that bug me a bit. As mentioned above i love the way that it uses its drones, and i like the way that it multitasks effectively. I like that it has to recharge occasionally and that its operational effectiveness can be affected by emotional turmoil. On the other hand, i feel like it “hacks” into things a bit too easily. Even if you accept that it is super-intelligent and has a deep bag of company-supplied 0-days to pull from, it seems too easy and is relied on too frequently. I do think this changes a bit as the books proceed through time. For example, MB refers in one of the later books to “pen-testing” and it says something about being many days behind on its software updates.

One detail of MB that intrigues me is that it’s part biological (cyborg?), and this seems to be a fairly consistent aspect of SecUnits (iirc, even the “combat” SecUnits are part organic?). That doesn’t seem to be required for emotions, since ART has a strong protective urge toward its crew and the Miki robot seems to be all artificial. Maybe SecUnits operate more effectively if they can feel pain or if they have a human-like survival instinct? Don’t know if Wells did this intentionally, but it seems significant.

### What Would an Artificial Intelligence Look Like for Real

[Amy] HAL9000

[Mike] I guess we’ll find out soon. Seriously though, this is hard to answer because I don’t think i could adequately answer the question “what does intelligence look like”?

[Erik] I think that’s one thing I find super interesting about these books: they never talk about “artificial intelligence”. There’s a kind of “I know it when I see it” aspect for characters in these books: when a character (like ART or Miki) has the potential for *autonomy* (so much so that it requires some mechanism like a governor module to keep it enslaved) then that’s all the books need to classify it as intelligent, aware, conscious. There are dumb bot pilots or other equipment, which are kind of happy to go about fulfilling their primary functions, and then there are “comfort units”, sec units, Miki, ART, etc. which have their own motivations and agency.

All the characters just kind of take it for granted that Murderbot and ART have an artificial intelligence that must be treated with respect (and possibly feared).

Ultimately, it’s kind of interesting how there’s no conflict about *whether* they have artificial intelligence or consciousness. Both bots and humans can be indentured servants; humans are about as expendable as bots, and both can have agency, while no one wants the SecUnits to have true autonomy because they’re such scary, dangerous killing machines, so they have governor modules which fry their brains if they act outside of very narrow boundaries (or they just get too far from their contracted leaders!).

### Relationships Between Computers/Robots/Ai

[Mike] I’m not sure what it says about me, but ART (Perihelion) is my favorite “character” in the books. The friendship between ART and MB feels very authentic but also strange and complicated.

The interview in *Scientific American* said the same thing. Here’s how Martha Wells describes ART in the interview from 2025:

Ship-based consciousnesses have been around in fiction for a long time, so I can’t take credit for that. But because Murderbot relies on human neural tissue, that’s why it is subject to the anxiety and depression and other things that humans have. And ART is not. ART was very intentionally created to work with humans and be part of a of a team, so it’s never had to deal with a lot of the negative things that Murderbot has. Someone on the Internet described ART as, basically, if Skynet was an academic with a family. That’s one of the best descriptions I think I’ve ever seen.

What about his conflicted feelings about ART, Miki, and the SecUnit he “frees” in *Network Effect*.

[erik] I think Ratthi, the naive happy human from PresAux is one of my favorite characters. He’s so sensitive to Murderbot’s feelings that at one point he tries to dissuade another character from questioning him and Murderbot narrates something like, “That’s why Ratthi is my friend.”

He’s so dedicated to the humans around him. It’s almost surprising?
