### 2001: A Space Odyssey

Arthur C Clarke and Stanley Kubrick (1968)

The simian(?) tribe finds the monolith and then vanquishes their enemies with some tools they figure out how to use. A long time later the monolith appears on the moon and as scientists are investigating it a radio signal is emitted.

Some time after that (a year?) the spacecraft Discovery One is going to Jupiter. There are loads of ponderous slow shots which I find really memorable but my wife thinks it’s one of the most boring movies ever made. But there’s a computer called HAL 9000 which performs most of the executive functions on their spaceship. It helps them run the ship, stay active, and even performs psychological and behavioral reviews of the astronauts. And then it starts trying to kill them!

And then the movie has a weird ending, from [wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2001%3A_A_Space_Odyssey#Plot):

At Jupiter, Bowman finds a third, much larger monolith orbiting the planet. He leaves Discovery in an EVA pod to investigate. He is pulled into a vortex of coloured light and observes bizarre astronomical phenomena and strange landscapes of unusual colours as he passes by. Finally he finds himself in a large neoclassical bedroom where he sees, and then becomes, older versions of himself: first standing in the bedroom, middle-aged and still in his spacesuit, then dressed in leisure attire and eating dinner, and finally as an old man lying in bed. A monolith appears at the foot of the bed, and as Bowman reaches for it, he is transformed into a foetus enclosed in a transparent orb of light floating in space above the Earth.

#### Commentary

A malevolent software entity that elects to kill humans and tries to frustrate their efforts to plot against it. It has a neutral and emotionless voice.

Human ethics cannot consistently and reliably guide its behavior, but until late in the film the humans just trust this technology, which is an extension of all human tools, going back millions of years.

Software is scary. Why? It’s technology that cannot be controlled? It’s technology whose capabilities cannot be fully comprehended?

Related, new OpenAI model was evaluated and found to be able to scheme (from [vox](https://www.vox.com/future-perfect/371827/openai-chatgpt-artificial-intelligence-ai-risk-strawberry)):

Evaluators who tested Strawberry found that it planned to deceive humans by making its actions seem innocent when they weren’t. The AI “sometimes instrumentally faked alignment” — meaning, alignment with the values and priorities that humans care about — and strategically manipulated data “in order to make its misaligned action look more aligned,” the system card says. It concludes that the AI “has the basic capabilities needed to do simple in-context scheming.”

### War Games

1983, directed by John Badham, starring Matthew Broderick and Ally Sheedy

Plot from [wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WarGames):

David Lightman, a bright but unmotivated Seattle high school student and hacker, uses his IMSAI 8080 computer and modem to access the school district's computer system and change the grades for himself and his friend and classmate, Jennifer Mack. Later, while war dialing numbers in Sunnyvale, California, to find a computer game company, he connects with a system that does not identify itself. Asking for games, he finds a list including chess, checkers, backgammon and poker, along with titles such as "Theaterwide Biotoxic and Chemical Warfare" and "Global Thermonuclear War", but cannot proceed further. Two hacker friends explain the concept of a backdoor password and suggest tracking down the Falken referenced in "Falken's Maze", the first game listed. David discovers that Stephen Falken was an early artificial-intelligence researcher, and guesses correctly that the name of Falken's deceased son (Joshua) is the password.

So then he initiates a simulation of "Global Thermonuclear War" but everyone at Norad thinks it’s really happening. Sidebar here: doesn’t Norad look like some kind of Star Wars space cruiser set? It looks great over there at Norad. Massive points for the walls of computers with blinking lights too.

Then the feds come after him, he gets taken to Cheyenne mountain and he gets imprisoned but escapes with some MacGuyvery and then he joins a school tour of Norad (they offer tours?).

Then he and Ally Sheedy go up to find Falken (who was reported dead) and they convince him that there’s an ongoing simulation which will threaten nuclear annihilation, but he’s very cynical. So then Falken kicks them out but then he picks them up in his helicopter (convenient!) and they fly to Norad and save the day!

WOPR stages a massive Soviet first strike with hundreds of missiles, submarines, and bombers. Believing the attack to be genuine, NORAD prepares to retaliate. Falken, David, and Jennifer convince military officials to delay the second strike and ride out the supposed attack until actual weapons impacts are confirmed. When the targeted American bases report back unharmed, NORAD prepares to cancel the retaliatory second strike. WOPR tries to launch the missiles on its own using a brute-force attack to obtain the launch codes. Without humans in the control centers as a safeguard using the two-man rule, the computer will trigger a mass launch. All attempts to log in and order WOPR to cancel the countdown fail. Disconnecting the computer is discussed and dismissed, as a fail-deadly mechanism will launch all weapons if the computer is disabled.

### Neuromancer

William Gibson (1984)

Plot: Case is a computer hacker, a “console cowboy,” and thief who was damaged so that he can’t go into virtual reality anymore, “the matrix.” He gets hired by a shadowy mercenary type figure who wants him to engage in a heist and they make him well enough to hack into the matrix again, but also put poison sacs in his body which they will repair if he does the job.

From [wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neuromancer#Plot):

Case is approached by Molly Millions, an augmented "razorgirl" and mercenary on behalf of a shadowy US ex-military officer named Armitage, who offers to cure Case for his services as a hacker. Case agrees, and his nervous system is repaired, though sacs of poison are placed in his blood vessels. If Case completes the job, Armitage will have the sacs removed; if not, they will burst and cripple him again.

### Better Living Through Algorithms

By Naomi Kritzer

Published May 2023

2024 Hugo Award Winner for Best Short Story

Plot: A new mobile app called Abelique “for better living” is used by everyone in the narrator’s social circle and there’s that feeling of virality: *suddenly everyone is talking about this thing*.

Sometimes, once you hear about a new thing, suddenly it’s just everywhere.

The narrator, Linnea, finds that her workplace starts pressuring her to use the app too ostensibly for productivity. After the app, the app starts getting involved in her life in a pushy, creepy way; she gets phone calls from other app users who have been recruited to encourage her to pursue her goals, drawing. Soon *she’s* making calls to other app users. The app doesn’t just charge people, but gives some people money and encourages them to quit their jobs. It’s really invasive in that surveillance tech way and the odd thing is that it seems to be actually making people’s lives *better*. She starts meeting up with people outside to draw and suddenly finds that she’s part of a community of live human beings.

Her boss starts pressuring her to stop using the app and then sounds out a security bulletin. Then, the story continues:

Keith’s worries were the start of a trend.

The “security bulletin” was followed by a series of increasingly paranoid news stories. None of them had figured out who was running it, and all of them used the adjective “shadowy” because that sounds like maybe monsters. Several of the articles included profiles of app users, which could have been fine, but one of them picked a formerly incarcerated person who was struggling with sobriety and found the app helpful because he’d become so reliant on an imposed routine while in prison. Another picked a woman who talked about how the app helped her “tune in to the vibrations of the universe”; the third presented an awkward composite rather than an actual human being. In other words, all those articles said, this app was for losers.

An article by her friend Margo comes out later with information about where the app comes from:

Abelique was run by an AI, built and run by a lab at Temple University in Philadelphia. The computer scientists at Temple had built the app and given the AI the goal of making people happier . . . and then just watched to see what happened.

…

The article kind of glossed over the ethics involved, but the former grad student emphasized that the question they always asked was, “which of two good things makes people happier” and not “can we make people unhappy.” “We already know how to make people unhappy,” she said. “Just look around you.”

Everything Abelique had told me to do suddenly made sense. Research showed that people were happier making fewer decisions, spending time outside, driving less, and spending less time on traditional social media. So the app gave me a weekly menu, sent me for walks, told me to take the train, and pestered me to read books instead of Twitter.

Ultimately, interest in the app fades over time and the narrator feels like something has been lost.

Interest in Abelique surged again, but something about knowing how all of it worked took some of the magic of the site away. More importantly, though—other people started finding ways to circumvent the rules. People started joining the app to try to sell us their multi-level-marketing schemes. Food threads started attracting posts about meal-replacement drinks, and it was quickly clear that someone was getting a payout for new customers of these gross fake shakes. One morning, my wake-up call was audio spam instead of a human voice, and that was it. I didn’t remove the app, but I started ignoring my wake-up call and my go-to-bed text, which fortunately meant the app didn’t try to make me do the phone tree. The less you used it, the less the app asked of you. In my free time in the evenings, I started binge-watching reality TV.

Narrator ends by drawing in the park by a former app user who contacts her outside of the app.

#### Questions

- Could it happen? Does it feel authentic or potentially real?

#### Commentary

The idea of an app that goes viral that promises life improvements feels super familiar. In addition this app is powered by AI and is incredibly invasive, like the way that Facebook listens to you passively via your phone.

And then that feeling of regret or wistfulness when that brief feeling of community fades away.

I like this story because it’s unsettling, it’s not easy to interpret what it means or how to respond, but that makes me reflect on my expectations for a story like this and then ask where those expectations come from.

Another possible case of a sympathetic AI is Becky Chambers’s *The Long Way to a Small Angry Planet*. One of the characters in the book falls in love with the ship’s AI (Lovelace) and goes to the lengths of trying to acquire a body for it (a “body kit”).

Mike’s Take on A Fire Upon the Deepby Vernor Vinge

I wanted to read this one because Vinge was part of the cohort of successful sci fi writers who came out of UC San Diego (Kim Stanley Robinson, David Brin, Catherynne Valente). Also, he passed away in March.

My review: Meh, not my thing. I generally dislike far-future sci fi or space opera. Vinge seems to have a talent for unintentionally funny language (eg, “Straumli perversion”, “skroderider”). I found the “Zones of Thought” stuff confusing and unrealistic. The most interesting human character is a guy assembled from spare parts? (Pham Nuwen).

Connections to software:

- A key plot point is the creation/release of a hyper-intelligence that seems to be malicious. A manifestation of *singularity*. Connections to current AI debates. Connects maybe to Erik’s thesis that technology is usually presented negatively or at least as a cautionary tale (although these “powers” are said to be usually benign).
  + Vinge doesn’t say much about what the “computers” look like in this world.
- There’s a Usenet-like messaging system (the book came out in ‘92), although it’s more sophisticated in terms of language translation and the scope of transmission. Also, less overt trolling.
- There is a species of alien (the “Tines”) that are basically hive-mind dog packs (can we work in the “dog paxos” joke?). Vinge explicitly refers to types of networking topology in describing how the collectives combine to make a single intelligence.
