### Software Freedom Conservancy

Opening keynote on Sunday, by Denver Gingerich: “What happens when hardware puts software freedom first? We built a router to find out.”

Denver Gingerich is the Director of Compliance at the Software Freedom Conservancy. This is a nonprofit organization that fights for free and open source software. According to their website:

Our mission is to ensure the right to repair, improve and reinstall software. We promote and defend these rights through fostering free and open source software (FOSS) projects, driving initiatives that actively make technology more inclusive, and advancing policy strategies that defend FOSS (such as copyleft).

Gingerich started by saying “Why are we here at this conference?” He said, we probably want to find out about “cool technology,” but we are also here to be empowered. Shifts in technology are empowering, but it’s hard to quantify power and thus optimize. This talk about power was echoed in the Hazel Weakly talk I went to the previous morning.

For an example of this, Gingerich talked about the openWRT One router, in use all throughout the conference, sponsored by the SFC. He started by saying that there are fancy routers on the market for upwards of $400, which are aggressively end-of-lifed by their vendors, who refuse to continue putting out software fixes for them. The Software Freedom Conservancy is also sponsoring projects like Etherpad, an open-source collaborative document editor.

And then he talked about all the lawsuits sponsored by the Software Freedom Conservancy against corporations that are shipping closed-source, proprietary software on top of Linux. He argued, “we are fighting for your rights.” Linux is licensed under the GPL which is informally a “copyleft license”. The Software Freedom Conservancy gives a definition of “copyleft licenses” as those with:

the intent to promote sharing (using copyright licensing to freely use and repair software). Software programs are copylefted when they utilize software with a copyleft license. These copyleft licenses are unique in that they seek to put large corporations, small companies, consumers, and hobbyists all on equal footing. The intention is for this to be achieved by granting everyone the exact same rights, permissions, and privileges to modify, improve, and/or include the software in their products.

There’s an idealism in here that I sometimes struggle to make jibe with reality. The Software Freedom Conservancy is winning lawsuits they sponsor, against all kinds of manufacturers: dishwashers, washing machines, televisions, cars.

The software that became openWRT was open-sourced as the result of a lawsuit Gingerish said. This idealism has a nostalgic quality to me, it takes me back to these warm and exciting days of 20 years when I was getting into Linux.

But on the other hand, if you take Linux, which is arguably the most successful computer operating system in history, and you tell people there’s an idealistic political philosophy under the surface I have a most people would go, “huh?” The analogy for me is if you walked into the hardware store, you hung out in the power tools section, and every time someone picked up a particular power drill, you told people, “Did you know there’s an ethos inspiring the work on this power drill? That the people who produce it want you to be free to repair, modify, or extend it? If you are making money off these modifications, though, you have to promise to give your modification plans away for free, back to the community.”

I think this analogy is a funny encapsulation of how the idealism sometimes feels. Most people I think would be like, “I just need a drill to fix something in my house.” It’s the toolness of the thing. A tool feels like it’s politically neutral. If you said to someone using the tool, “we are fighting for your rights as a tool user,” they may be confused. On the other hand, I bet almost every tool user would be like, “yes! I like that idea.” Maybe it’s the pervading culture that makes this perspective seem out of square with reality: we’re used to *buying tools*. We’re used to having *few rights* with respect to the usage of tools.

### Leslie Lamport: Closing Keynote “Coding Isn’t Programming”

There were people behind us who’d never heard of Leslie Lamport. I don’t know how representative this is: they seemed young, but they saw his association with Microsoft and were blowing it off like, “pffft. This guy’s from Microsoft? He’s going to tell us why we should care about windows or Microsoft products?!”

Over the course of the next roughly 45 minutes, Lamport offered chunks of an argument that appears in much longer form in his book A Science of Concurrent Programming (a PDF of which you can find on his website for free). The argument is:

- Programmers care too much about programming languages, hacking, “coding”
- Instead, they should care about *algorithms*
- To validate the correctness of their algorithms, they should rely on math.
- That may sound scary, but math in this case means, “write and validate abstract programs”.
- An “abstract program” is where most of the real-world concerns have been stripped away. It’s not a program you could use to solve the actual problem you have, but a *model* or interpretation of your program.

He used a lot of the terminology that he’s been using over the last 20 years to talk about abstract programs and TLA+:

- An abstract program is a sequence of states
- A state is an assignment of a variable
- A step is a transition from one state to the next

His argument is that while programmers are taught to *code* they are not taught how to *abstract*. He said at one point, maybe TLA+ is not something all programmers will be able to understand. I wanted to write it down, but it was an interesting and alarming idea for me.

After this, he gave another short talk called “Why Programs Should have bugs.” Lamport said he started writing programs in 1957 and that we’ve gotten more and more complex programs, based on libraries, and things have actually gotten harder to use as we’ve built upon layers of complexity.

He ultimately said that a program can only have bugs if there is a precise description of what it should do. However, few programs have such a description.

This is related to the above: do we even bother giving a precise description to our programs?

When it came time for questions someone asked him if he saw more people picking up tools like TLA+ and he said that he had an intern who was great, but when that intern got a job, he was told, “stop doing all that TLA+. It’s extra work”!

The problem Lamport said is that you have to stop and think about what you’re writing. You can’t just hack it out.

I wrote this line down: “Thinking is a very hard sell. Most people would rather fight than think.”

### My Lamport Meeting and a Question

I waited in line for about 30 minutes to ask him a question. I felt kind of bad, a long line, and he looked fatigued. The man is 84 years old! There were people in front of me kind of spitballing questions, but I had a question I really wanted to ask him.

A Science of Concurrent Programming, he had this line that stuck in my head. He says something like (paraphrasing): "fancy types allow you to express a type like 'non-negative integer' which keeps you from dividing by zero, but it's too much effort to create and work with fancy types and you don't even need it because you can use assertions instead." This is the justification for why TLA+ is untyped and how you can still validate abstract programs with it.

So this line about fancy types had been stuck in my brain since I read it, so when I got a chance to ask him a question, I said, "I'm a programmer and still hung up on languages so I have a dumb question for you." I then repeated his argument about fancy types back to him and then I asked him: "If you had it to do over again and you were going to write TLA+ today, would you reconsider using fancy types?"

In response Lamport said, "the CoC people argued with me so much about this years ago. They fought me so hard on this.” Ultimately, he said, “I would not do it any differently." And then he said this thing that I’ve been thinking about ever since. He said, "The math that programmers use is so basic that you don't need fancy types to express it."

What does that mean! “The math programmers use is so basic”!

Well, I think it echoes something in his book, where he writes:

"Mathematically the behavior of a digital system or abstract program is an infinite cardinal sequence of states where each state is an assignment of values/sets to variables."

I think when he told me that "programmers only use basic math", I'm guessing he meant to say, "you only need basic math to represent what's happening in programs". In other places I believe he states that that's ZMF + the Axiom of Choice and also the temporal quantifiers "always" and "eventually". For him this is “basic” math, but it’s like “a sequence of states”, some sets, some boolean operators and proof operators, like implies, and some temporal quantifiers.

Using these things, you can demonstrate correctness of almost any interesting algorithm, including fantastically byzantine concurrent, multithreaded, confusing programs.

Did you know that Lamport originally conceived of TLA+ as a language for doing proofs only? He didn’t even think you could build an actual model checker out of it and he told a colleague it was impossible. He was happy to be proved wrong when TLC (the model checker) was produced.
