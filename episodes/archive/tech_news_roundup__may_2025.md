### 3. “Health bars and power ups: The 'freaky and unpleasant' world when video games leak into the physical realm”

[[Link](https://www.bbc.com/future/article/20250409-game-transfer-phenomenon-the-people-who-see-video-games-in-the-physical-world)]

This is an article about how people mentally transfer elements of videogames into the world around them, how the “virtual experience spills over into reality”. From the piece:

One study participant she interviewed reported seeing health indicator bars like those in the role-playing game World of Warcraft floating above their companions' heads. Another spoke of lapses in concentration after not being able to stop "seeing" images from a game. Others said colours in the real world seemed transformed, and began to mimic the colours of a game world they had recently played in.

Angelica Ortiz de Gortari is the researcher who coined the term and she says:

“The more realistic the video game world is, the more likely players are to confuse the game world and the real world”

This article struck me because I’ve been playing a lot of tetris lately. I’ve been playing the game in order to clean out my mind after work. Sidebar: would you say that Tetris is “realistic”?

Anyway, after playing Tetris the next day sometimes I’ll be talking to people and involuntarily in my head organizing tetriminos as they fall. And I feel kind of bad like, “I should be paying attention instead of playing head tetris!” Also, I’m not getting any credit for clearing these lines…

Two things confused me in this article: 1) they say it is uncommon, but it may also be common(?) and 2) it “may be dangerous”? How would it be dangerous: is it a form of hallucination? The rare vs common thin appears in this paragraph:

In total, Ortiz de Gortari has recorded GTP experiences among gamers related to more than 400 titles of all kinds. Her largest on the subject to date, published in 2024, involved the participation of 623 Chinese gamers, both male and female. The results suggest that between 82% and 96% of those gamers have experienced some form of GTP.

It’s described as an involuntary change of perception, but the danger may be here:

Ortiz de Gortari's studies suggest that GTP induces distress and dysfunction for around half of those gamers who say they have experienced it, with confusion, hyper-vigilance and irrationality among the symptoms. For others the only notable response may be a feeling of embarrassment that their game-play has spilled over into "real life".

### 4. Squared Blunder: Google Engineer retracts preprint written with AI

[‘Squared blunder’: Google engineer withdraws preprint after getting called out for using AI – Retraction Watch](https://retractionwatch.com/2025/04/24/google-ai-engineer-withdraws-arxiv-preprint-tortured-phrases-genai/)

In March 2025, sleuth Guillaume Cabanac, creator of the Problematic Paper Screener, pointed out in a PubPeer comment the paper included several tortured phrases. These phrases indicate AI use and occur when large language models try to find synonyms for common phrases. In Awasthi’s paper, “linear regression” became “straight relapse,” and “error rate” became “blunder rate,” among others.

My personal favorite “mean squared error” becomes “mean squared blunder”

Google guy: “Anurag Awasthi is an Engineering Lead specializing in AI infrastructure, cloud computing, and machine learning.”

### 5. Luddite Lessons: How to Survive the AI Revolution

[[Link](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2025/04/21/how-to-survive-the-ai-revolution)]

This article gives context for the Luddite battles in England in the 19th century. The luddites were textile workers who protested and destroyed automated textile machines, like the steam-powered looms that wove cotton thread into fabrics.

They hanged nine people responsible for breaking these machines in riots.

The Luddites grasped an essential truth: the factory system threatened their artisanal economy and livelihoods.

Q: Is software engineering an artisanal economy and livelihood?

Cotton manufacturing had two stages: spinning fiber yarn and then yarn into fabric. Inventors had mechanized the spinning part:

…but weaving initially remained too complex to automate. This technological imbalance actually increased demand for hand-loom weavers, whose numbers grew in Britain from 37,000 to 208,000 between 1780 and 1812. Until about 1800, these weavers, many of whom worked from their homes, enjoyed rising incomes.

In 1785, a power loom was invented. Then in the early-1800s, it was improved upon with steam-powered versions.

This development, combined with slumping global textile demand during the Napoleonic Wars, devastated weavers’ livelihoods. From 1804 to 1810, their incomes plunged by more than 40%.

This history lesson is all a preamble to talking about AI. The writer argues that Luddites and our current era with AI is an apt comparison, given even by people who may be described as techno-optimists. This is mostly a piece about Economics and most of the work talked about and the people interviewed are from that field. The article states:

When AI automates human skills outright, [MIT Economist Erik Brynjolfsson] warns “machines become better substitutes for human labor,” while “workers lose economic and political bargaining power, and become increasingly dependent on those who control the technology.” In this environment, tech giants – which own and develop AI – accumulate vast wealth and power, while most workers are left without leverage or a path to improving their conditions. Brynjolfsson termed this dystopian outcome: “the Turing Trap,” after the computing pioneer Alan Turing.

Q: What do we think about this name “The Turing Trap”?

Another MIT Economist David Autor, brought up a 2023 study,

Showing that certain highly trained radiologists, when using AI tools, produced diagnoses that were less accurate, in part because they gave too much weight to inaccurate AI results. “The tool itself is very good, yet doctors perform worse with it,” he said…

The broader goal, he argued, should be to enable workers without advanced degrees to take on high-value decision-making tasks. “But that message has to filter all the way down to the question of: How do benchmark success?” he said. “I think it’s feasible but it’s not simple.”

Sidebar: this happened to me this week. I was shipping a PR with a kind of throwaway script in it. It didn’t core production behavior: I just wanted a convenient way to run something a few times and then never again. So I mostly let copilot write the script without thinking about it, and it was calling functions from my program with the wrong arguments! So I let copilot take the wheel and shipped something pretty bad in that case!

The article ends with a statement from this same economist David Autor:

“We would have handled this challenge better in the 1970s than we are now. Ironically, I have less faith in our ability to manage it today than I would have had when we were a lower-tech society. This is probably a bad moment for AI to appear.”

### 6. Yahoo Wants to Buy Chrome

[[Link](https://www.theverge.com/policy/655975/yahoo-search-web-browser-prototype-google-trial-antitrust-chrome)]

There was a legal finding last year and early this year that Google holds an illegal monopoly for search and online advertising technology and that they stifled competition in that space.

The case is about how they have this marketing money-printer in the form of Search, the Chrome browser (because people usually just type into the browser bar to search), maps, YouTube and other things. The thing with maps has been annoying me: I will never in my life visit a Wendy’s. And yet Google likes to give me directions to places like, “that’s right by the Wendy’s!” It always feels subtle and dystopian to me…

Anyway, here’s from an AP piece:

The Justice Department lawyers argued that Google built and maintained dominant market positions in a technology trifecta used by website publishers to sell ad space on their webpages, as well as the technology that advertisers use to get their ads in front of consumers, and the ad exchanges that conduct automated auctions in fractions of a second to match buyer and seller.

[The judge in the suit concluded] the company has been abusing its power to stifle competition to the detriment of online publishers forced to rely on its network for revenue.

The lawsuit against them started in 2023, initiated by the US Department of Justice and now that’s it’s been decided, they have to have a “remedies trial” to make suggestions on what to do about it. So then they just started this 3-week “remedies trial” process and the Department of Justice’s suggested remedies so far from the trial that are in the news are “sell chrome” and “don’t make deals with Apple to make Chrome the default.”

Legacy search brand Yahoo has been working on its own web browser prototype, and says it would like to buy Google’s Chrome if the company is forced by a court to sell it.

Yahoo seems to agree that owning a web browser is a very important portal for search, and is looking to run its own, either through developing or buying one. Yahoo Search General Manager Brian Provost testified that about 60% of search queries are done through a web browser; many people search directly from the address bar. That’s why, Provost testified, since last summer Yahoo has been “actively internally developing a prototype of a browser” to understand what it would need to do to put one out in the market. He added that Yahoo is in “ongoing” discussions with other companies about buying a browser, though he would not publicly state which ones it’s in talks with.

Also a surprising ending describing how Provost believes that buying Chrome would cost tens of billions of dollars, but they can get the funding from the owner of Yahoo: Apollo Global Management.

Apollo actually owns a browser brand that was at the center of a different antitrust case, but Provost said he wouldn’t consider it an active browser. The brand’s name? NetScape.

### 7. Novel color via stimulation of individual photoreceptors at population scale

[[link](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.adu1052#sec-2)]

From the abstract:

We introduce a principle, Oz, for displaying color imagery: directly controlling the human eye’s photoreceptor activity via cell-by-cell light delivery. Theoretically, novel colors are possible through bypassing the constraints set by the cone spectral sensitivities and activating M cone cells exclusively. In practice, we confirm a partial expansion of colorspace toward that theoretical ideal. Attempting to activate M cones exclusively is shown to elicit a color beyond the natural human gamut, formally measured with color matching by human subjects.

From the paper:

We name this new color “olo,” with the ideal version of olo defined as pure M activation. Subjects report that olo in our prototype system appears blue-green of unprecedented saturation, when viewed relative to a neutral gray background. Subjects find that they must desaturate olo by adding white light before they can achieve a color match with the closest monochromatic light, which lies on the boundary of the gamut, unequivocal proof that olo lies beyond the gamut.
